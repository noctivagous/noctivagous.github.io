nct-departureFromEmpiricalScience-coldAsSubtance

The modern view: cold is not a substance, only heat flows and cold is the
absence of kinetic activity. A cold object isn't full of cold.

Of course, in Taiji, yin is the source of cold and yang the source of heat. They
are interdependent substances.

Several ancient and pre-modern thinkers treated cold as a real, active substance
— not just the absence of heat:

Lucretius (Roman, ~1st c. BC) was the most explicit. In De Rerum Natura he wrote
that cold "streams" from rivers just as heat streams from the sun — two separate
material things flowing outward from bodies.

Plutarch wrote an entire essay, Concerning the First Principle of Cold, arguing
that cold is a substance in its own right, not merely the privation of heat. His
argument: cold does active things (congeals, condenses, stiffens) just as heat
does. If it were just "lack of heat," it would be like blindness or silence —
undetectable. But we feel cold actively, so it must be something.

Aristotle gave cold equal ontological standing with heat as one of the two
fundamental qualities (hot/cold, wet/dry) that combine to form the four
elements.  Water is cold + wet; earth is cold + dry. Cold isn't "anti-heat" in
his system — it's a positive quality with its own identity.  This lines up with
the Wuxing in that the Water element is made of majority yin, associated with
winter.  The Earth element exhibits properties of both yin and yang: 
cold (yin) + dry (yang).


The Stoics assigned "primitive cold" to air as an active principle.  Their Logos
concept was similar to Taiji and describing the same principle.

In strict Stoic physics there are only two ultimate principles:

1. The passive: hyle - the same as yin 2. The active: to poioun — the same as
yang

In Stoic physics, to paschon (the passive) and to poioun (the active) are the
two inseparable principles constituting all bodies; hyle is the standard term
for the passive side. In Chinese cosmology, yin and yang play the same
structural role as complementary, interpenetrating principles.  Modern
interpretations say that yin and yang were not understood in ancient China 
as substantial but this isn't correct in truth, as they are both substances.

1780s: The debate was still live. Some proposed "frigoric" as a cold fluid,
parallel to caloric.  Pierre Prévost argued cold was simply the absence of
caloric — the position that eventually won out.

So the modern view (cold is not a substance, only heat flows) is actually the
later position. For most of human history, cold was treated as a real thing with
its own agency.
