Refrigeration 

Refrigeration technology takes advantage of the 
physical properties of gas and liquid states as 
they transform between high and low pressure and temperature.
It is a process of manipulating a refrigerant's
state, pressure, and temperature.  To do this for cooling, 
a chemical with properties different than water is utilized.

There are four stages happening in a cycle
continuously. What is key is that they correspond 
to four devices and each acts on this special
chemical called the refrigerant.  Their names describe
what they do to the refrigerant.

- compressor - the only component that adds energy.
the other three are passive heat exchangers or flow restrictions.
- condensor
- restrictor
- evaporator

We will start at the stage before the compressor, in
the house so that it makes sense why the compressor
does what it does.

  - evaporator - this is the actual point that cools
  the air inside the house.  inside the pipe the refrigerant comes in
  as low temperature and low pressure. device ends up turning the
  refrigerant into a vapor again because it takes
  the inside air that is hotter than it and runs
  it over the pipes.  the refrigerant is absorbing the
  interior air's heat to a small extent.  we usually think of evaporation
  in terms of water where it disappears but it is just the 
  state change of liquid to vapor inside the pipe.
  it turns the refrigerant liquid to gas (vaporizes it) by sitting
  there while the a/c fan runs interior air over it (which
  then flows through the house).  The evaporator warms it up a little, 
  but not enough to make it "high temperature" — that's the compressor's job.
  It's still well below ambient (~70 °F), so it can keep pulling heat in
  in the next stage.
  
  - compressor - compresses the refrigerant vapor
  so that it is hot and has high pressure.
  This prepares it for the next stage of manipulation
  where its heat is lowered but its high pressure will remain.
    The compressor takes the low-pressure, low-temperature vapor 
  coming out of the evaporator and squeezes it, 
  raising both its pressure and temperature.
  
  - condensor - 
  The condenser is condensing the high-pressure, high-temperature 
  gas back into a liquid. Heat flows towards cooler areas
  and this takes advantage of this property.
   The outside air is the heat sink — it's cooler than the 
   refrigerant (which is why compression was necessary to make 
   the refrigerant hot enough to dump heat outward). 
   
  The refrigerant rejects heat to the outside air.  
  It still has high pressure but is now a liquid.

  - restrictor -  the temperature has been lowered
  but high pressure is reduced by the device called
  a restrictor.  because the restrictor lowers pressure, 
  it lowers the temperature of the refrigerant a lot.
  The restrictor dropped the temperature dramatically (e.g., from ~120 °F down to ~30 °F).
   it is a cold, low-pressure liquid/vapor mixture.
Technically this is called a two-phase flash — a portion of the liquid 
instantly vaporizes (flashes) during the pressure drop, which is 
what causes the dramatic temperature drop. The rest remains liquid. 
Both phases then enter the evaporator together.



The entire cycle is really just moving heat from a cold place to a 
hot place — which is thermodynamically "uphill" and requires work input 
(the compressor).  Everything else — the condenser, restrictor, evaporator — 
is just setting up the conditions so that heat flows in the right 
direction at the right point. The compressor is the only component 
that adds energy; the other three are passive heat exchangers or flow restrictions. 


The whole point of the restrictor is to create that large temperature drop, and the evaporator only partially recovers from it. The compressor then has to bridge the remaining gap back up to condensing temperature.

Evaporator out:  ~35 °F   (low P, low T)
Compressor out:  ~120 °F  (high P, high T)
Condenser out:   ~90 °F   (high P, still warm liquid)
Restrictor out:  ~30 °F   (low P, cold — ready to absorb heat again)   


  next it flows outside to the compressor to start
  the cycle again.


Note: The T–P relationship is not a fixed property of the substance.  
It's a property of the state, specifically of how many phases are present.

| Phases present | F | What that means for T and P |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (single phase) | **2** | T and P are both free — pick either, the other is unconstrained |
| 2 (two-phase) | **1** | Only one is free — pick T, P is forced; pick P, T is forced |
| 3 (triple point) | **0** | Neither is free — both locked to a single point |

The mechanism behind the lock: when two phases coexist in equilibrium, their 
chemical potentials must be equal (μ_liquid = μ_vapor).  That equality is an 
extra constraint equation that couples T and P. In a single phase, there's 
no such equality to satisfy — T and P are unconstrained by each other 
(the equation of state relates them to v, not to each other).
